Paper Review - Data-Efficient Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement Learning
Paper-Review
A review of goal-conditioned HRL, off-policy correction, and high-level actor-critic learning.
공개

2026년 11월 15일

Introduction

This post is about goal-conditioned hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL), based on Data-Efficient Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (Nachum et al., 2018). I will focus on how the lower-level and higher-level policies are trained, especially the off-policy correction method proposed in the paper, called HIRO.

1. Goal-Conditioned Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning

The lower-level policy receives a goal as an additional input and learns actions using a reward based on that goal.

The higher-level policy operates over a longer time scale than the lower-level policy. It generates a goal every \(c\) steps and learns which goals lead to higher environmental rewards.

In this paper, the goal represents a desired relative change in the state. To keep the absolute target unchanged as the state changes, the goal transition is defined as

\[ h(s_t,g_t,s_{t+1})=s_t+g_t-s_{t+1}. \]

The intrinsic reward for the lower-level policy is

\[ r(s_t,g_t,a_t,s_{t+1})=-\|s_t+g_t-s_{t+1}\|_2. \]

Therefore, the lower-level policy receives a higher reward (a smaller distance penalty) when the next state is closer to the desired state \(s_t+g_t\).

The lower-level Q-function takes both the state and the goal as inputs, along with the low-level action:

\[ Q^{\mathrm{lo}}_\theta(s_t,g_t,a_t). \]

The lower-level actor generates an action \(a_t=\mu^{\mathrm{lo}}_\phi(s_t,g_t)\) and learns to maximize

\[ Q^{\mathrm{lo}}_\theta\left(s_t,g_t,\mu^{\mathrm{lo}}_\phi(s_t,g_t)\right). \]

For higher-level training, the experience collected over \(c\) lower-level steps can be summarized as

\[ (s_t,g_t,R_{t:t+c-1},s_{t+c}), \]

where \(R_{t:t+c-1}=\sum_{i=t}^{t+c-1}R_i\) is the accumulated environmental reward over that period. The higher-level policy can learn from these longer transitions rather than making a new goal decision at every step.

2. Off-Policy Correction

The lower-level policy can reuse past transitions for off-policy learning. However, the higher-level policy faces an additional problem: the lower-level policy changes during training.

A goal that produced a particular sequence of actions under an old lower-level policy may produce different actions under the current lower-level policy. Therefore, using the original high-level goal with past experience can make higher-level training inconsistent.

One possible approach is importance sampling. We can compare the probability of the recorded low-level actions under the current policy with their probability under the past behavior policy:

\[ w_t=\prod_{i=t}^{t+c-1} \frac{\mu^{\mathrm{lo}}(a_i\mid s_i,g_i)} {\mu^{\mathrm{lo}}_\beta(a_i\mid s_i,g_i)}. \]

Here, \(\mu^{\mathrm{lo}}_\beta\) is the lower-level behavior policy that generated the past experience. In the alternative importance-based formulation discussed in the appendix, the high-level target can be written conceptually as

\[ y_t=\mathbb E_\beta\left[ w_t\left(R_{t:t+c-1}+\gamma\max_g Q^{\mathrm{hi}}(s_{t+c},g)\right) \right], \]

and the corresponding critic loss is

\[ L(\theta)=\mathbb E_\beta\left[ \left(Q^{\mathrm{hi}}_\theta(s_t,g_t)-y_t\right)^2 \right]. \]

Ideally, importance weighting corrects the difference between the two policies. However, multiplying probability ratios across a trajectory can lead to very small or very large weights. As the trajectory becomes longer, the resulting estimator can have high variance. This is an alternative correction considered in the paper, not the goal re-labeling procedure used in HIRO.

Therefore, the paper proposes goal re-labeling rather than relying on direct importance weighting.

2.1 Goal Re-labeling

The main idea is to replace the original goal with a new goal that makes the previously observed low-level actions likely under the current lower-level policy.

The re-labeled goal is selected as

\[ \tilde g_t =\arg\max_{\tilde g_t} \prod_{i=t}^{t+c-1} \mu^{\mathrm{lo}}(a_i\mid s_i,\tilde g_i), \]

where the intermediate goals \(\tilde g_i\) are determined from the candidate \(\tilde g_t\) by the goal transition function.

In other words, we search for a goal under which the current lower-level policy would have been likely to produce the recorded actions.

Although the actor is deterministic, exploration noise makes the behavior policy stochastic. Under Gaussian exploration noise, we can write

\[ a_i\sim\mathcal N\left(\mu^{\mathrm{lo}}_\phi(s_i,\tilde g_i),\sigma^2I\right). \]

If the variance is fixed, its log-probability is proportional to

\[ \log\mu^{\mathrm{lo}}(a_i\mid s_i,\tilde g_i) \propto-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \left\|a_i-\mu^{\mathrm{lo}}_\phi(s_i,\tilde g_i)\right\|_2^2. \]

Therefore, maximizing the probability of the whole action sequence is equivalent to minimizing

\[ \sum_{i=t}^{t+c-1} \left\|a_i-\mu^{\mathrm{lo}}_\phi(s_i,\tilde g_i)\right\|_2^2. \]

Instead of searching over every possible goal, the paper approximates this optimization using 10 candidate goals: the original goal, the observed state difference \(s_{t+c}-s_t\), and eight randomly sampled goals from a Gaussian centered at \(s_{t+c}-s_t\). The candidate with the highest likelihood is selected.

The resulting re-labeled transition is stored or used for higher-level off-policy training. This correction is approximate; it does not guarantee that the old and current lower-level policies are identical.

3. High-Level Critic

To learn which goals are useful, the higher-level policy needs a Q-function:

\[ Q^{\mathrm{hi}}_\theta(s_t,g_t). \]

The critic predicts the expected future environmental reward from selecting goal \(g_t\) in state \(s_t\).

For a high-level transition, the conceptual Bellman target is

\[ y_t=R_{t:t+c-1} +\gamma\max_g Q^{\mathrm{hi}}(s_{t+c},g). \]

The high-level critic minimizes the difference between its Q-value prediction and the target:

\[ L(\theta)=\mathbb E_{\mathcal D}\left[ \left(Q^{\mathrm{hi}}_\theta(s_t,\tilde g_t)-y_t\right)^2 \right]. \]

Here, \(\mathcal D\) is the replay buffer, and \(\tilde g_t\) is the re-labeled goal used for off-policy training.

The transition already contains the observed state \(s_{t+c}\), so we do not need to predict every possible state at that time. The unknown rewards after \(s_{t+c}\) are instead estimated by bootstrapping from the critic’s Q-value.

4. High-Level Actor

The higher-level actor generates a goal rather than a low-level action:

\[ g_t=\mu^{\mathrm{hi}}_\phi(s_t). \]

The critic evaluates the goal generated by the actor:

\[ Q^{\mathrm{hi}}_\theta\left(s_t,\mu^{\mathrm{hi}}_\phi(s_t)\right). \]

The actor updates its parameters in the direction that increases this value:

\[ \phi\leftarrow\phi+ \eta\nabla_\phi Q^{\mathrm{hi}}_\theta\left(s_t,\mu^{\mathrm{hi}}_\phi(s_t)\right). \]

Here, the gradient passes through the goal generated by the actor using the chain rule. In this way, the actor learns to generate goals that the critic evaluates more highly.

During experience collection, random noise is added to the generated goals to encourage exploration. The resulting trajectories are stored in the replay buffer, where they can later be used for critic training with off-policy correction.

5. Bellman Bootstrapping and TD3

The remaining problem is how to estimate the future Q-value in the Bellman target without explicitly evaluating every possible future goal.

The paper uses TD3, an actor-critic method that learns Q-values through Bellman bootstrapping. The target actor proposes a goal for the observed next high-level state \(s_{t+c}\), and the target critics evaluate that goal.

With two target critics, the bootstrapped target can be written schematically as

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde g_{t+c} &=\mu^{\mathrm{hi}}_{\bar\phi}(s_{t+c})+\epsilon,\\ y_t &=R_{t:t+c-1} +\gamma\min_{k\in\{1,2\}} Q^{\mathrm{hi}}_{\bar\theta_k}(s_{t+c},\tilde g_{t+c}), \end{aligned} \]

where \(\epsilon\) denotes clipped target-policy smoothing noise, and the bars denote target networks. This target is for a non-terminal transition; at a terminal state, the future-value term is omitted.

Taking the smaller of the two target Q-values helps reduce overestimation. The two critics are trained using the Bellman target, while the target networks are updated gradually through soft updates. TD3 also delays actor and target-network updates relative to critic updates.

Therefore, the higher-level policy can learn the value of its goals using recorded transitions and bootstrapped future values, without explicitly modeling all possible future trajectories.
