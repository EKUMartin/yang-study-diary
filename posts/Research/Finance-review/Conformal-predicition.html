Introduction

This post is an introduction to conformal prediction and how it can be used in finance.

Let’s first talk about what conformal prediction is.

When we want to make a prediction about something in the future, we might wonder how certain this prediction is. One way to statistically evaluate this uncertainty is to use a prediction interval rather than just a point prediction.

To build a prediction interval using split conformal prediction, we need to calculate how much error the model makes. Here, we use past data. We first split the data into training and calibration sets. The training set is used to train the model, and the calibration set is used to calculate prediction errors and eventually construct the prediction interval. Let’s now look at how to get the interval through equations.

Conformal Prediction

\[ \mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n+1}\in\hat{C}_n(X_{n+1})\right)\geq 1-\alpha. \]

Here, \(Y_{n+1}\) is the actual future value, and \(\hat{C}_n(X_{n+1})\) is the prediction interval. What we want is for the probability that the actual future value falls within the prediction interval to be at least \(1-\alpha\). This is a marginal coverage guarantee under the required assumptions, not a guarantee for every individual prediction.

The first step is to find the rank of the future prediction error relative to past calibration errors. One important property we need is that, under exchangeability, the future error’s rank is equally likely to occupy each possible position (assuming no ties, or using random tie-breaking).

First, we calculate the absolute prediction errors on the calibration set:

\[ R_i=\left|Y_i-\hat{f}_{\mathrm{train}}(X_i)\right|, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n. \]

Here, \(n\) is the number of calibration observations, and \(\hat{f}_{\mathrm{train}}\) is the model fitted using the separate training set. The adjusted quantile can be calculated from the following equation:

\[ \hat{q}_n=\operatorname{Quantile}\left( \frac{\left\lceil(1-\alpha)(n+1)\right\rceil}{n}; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{R_i} \right). \]

Here, \(\delta_{R_i}\) denotes a point mass at \(R_i\), so the second argument represents the empirical distribution of the calibration errors. Equivalently, \(\hat{q}_n\) is the \(\lceil(1-\alpha)(n+1)\rceil\)-th smallest calibration error. If this rank is \(n+1\), we take \(\hat{q}_n=+\infty\).

We have \(n+1\) because the unknown future error must also be accounted for in the ranking. The adjustment helps ensure that the interval achieves coverage of at least \(1-\alpha\) when the exchangeability condition holds.

One more important property is exchangeability. As the name suggests, exchangeability means that the joint distribution of the observations does not change under permutations. It is weaker than assuming the observations are independent and identically distributed.

Now, we have what we need to calculate the interval.

\[ \hat{C}_n(x)=\left[ \hat{f}_{\mathrm{train}}(x)-\hat{q}_n, \ \hat{f}_{\mathrm{train}}(x)+\hat{q}_n \right]. \]

This is the prediction interval we need. The function \(\hat{f}_{\mathrm{train}}(x)\) gives the predicted value, and \(\hat{q}_n\) determines the margin around that prediction using calibration errors.

Under the exchangeability assumption, this interval satisfies the coverage condition above. The method that uses separate training and calibration sets is called split conformal prediction.

Full Conformal Prediction

There is also full conformal prediction, which does not require a separate calibration set. Instead, for each possible future value \(y\) at a new input \(x\), we include the candidate pair \((x,y)\) in the data, fit the model, and calculate the absolute errors:

\[ R_i^{(x,y)} =\left|Y_i-\hat{f}_{n,(x,y)}(X_i)\right|, \qquad i=1,\ldots,n, \]

\[ R_{n+1}^{(x,y)} =\left|y-\hat{f}_{n,(x,y)}(x)\right|. \]

Here, \(\hat{f}_{n,(x,y)}\) is the model fitted using the original \(n\) observations and the candidate pair \((x,y)\). We compare the candidate error with the errors from the original observations and include \(y\) in the prediction set if it passes the adjusted rank threshold. The fitting procedure must treat the observations symmetrically for the usual exchangeability argument to apply.

Unlike split conformal prediction, full conformal prediction generally requires refitting the model for different candidate values, so it is more computationally expensive.

Application to Finance

In finance, we are curious about a portfolio’s future return, but it is only a prediction because we cannot be certain about the future. Therefore, we may apply conformal prediction to quantify uncertainty in portfolio return predictions and help evaluate the risk of losing money.

However, we cannot directly assume that the standard conformal coverage guarantee holds for financial data. Why? One important condition is exchangeability. Stock returns can be dependent over time, and their distributions may change with market conditions. Therefore, the exchangeability condition may not hold.

To address this problem, we may consider methods that account for time dependence or changing market conditions, such as rolling or adaptive calibration. However, using recent data or accounting for a trend does not, by itself, guarantee exchangeability or the original coverage level. The coverage properties must be justified under the assumptions of the method being used and checked empirically.

If we can construct and evaluate suitable prediction intervals for portfolio returns, we can compare the intervals across portfolios and choose one according to our return expectations and risk preferences. However, choosing the most favorable interval does not guarantee a profit, and selecting among many portfolios may require additional care to maintain coverage guarantees.

How to Apply Conformal Prediction to Financial Data

To apply conformal prediction to financial data, we first need to consider the time dependence of financial returns. Since market conditions change over time, past observations may not follow the same distribution as future observations. Therefore, simply shuffling the data does not guarantee exchangeability.

One possible approach is to use a rolling calibration window.

First, we train a prediction model using historical financial data. Then, instead of randomly splitting the data, we preserve the chronological order and use more recent observations for calibration.

We calculate prediction errors from the calibration period and use them to construct a prediction interval for the next portfolio return. As new observations become available, we update the calibration window and calculate the interval again.

In this way, the prediction interval can reflect more recent market conditions. However, rolling calibration alone does not guarantee the original coverage probability.

Another approach is Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI), proposed by Gibbs and Candès (2021).

Instead of using a fixed miscoverage level, ACI adjusts the calibration level based on whether recent observations fall inside the prediction intervals. This allows the method to adapt to changes in the data distribution.

For example, if realized returns fall outside the predicted intervals too frequently, ACI can adjust the calibration level to produce wider intervals. If the intervals are unnecessarily conservative, the calibration level can be adjusted in the opposite direction.

ACI provides theoretical guarantees for long-run coverage frequency under its online updating framework, but this does not mean that every prediction interval has the desired conditional coverage.

There is also Ensemble Batch Prediction Intervals (EnbPI), proposed by Xu and Xie (2021). This method uses ensemble predictions and sequentially updated prediction errors to construct intervals for time-series data without requiring exchangeability. Its approximate coverage guarantees depend on additional assumptions.

After constructing the prediction intervals, we can apply them to portfolio return predictions.

For example, instead of selecting a portfolio only because it has a high predicted return, we can also consider the lower bound of its prediction interval. A portfolio with a high predicted return but a very low lower bound may involve greater downside uncertainty.

Finally, we need to evaluate the method using historical out-of-sample data. We can compare the actual coverage rate with the target coverage rate and examine how the interval width changes under different market conditions.

We should also consider transaction costs, market liquidity, and the possibility that selecting a portfolio based on its prediction interval may affect the original coverage guarantee.

Therefore, conformal prediction can be used as a tool for evaluating uncertainty in financial return predictions, but its reliability depends on the calibration method, the assumptions, and the market conditions.
