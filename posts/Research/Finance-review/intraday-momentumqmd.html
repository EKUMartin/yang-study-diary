Paper Review - Market Intraday Momentum

Finance
Paper-Review
A methodology-focused review of market intraday momentum, predictive regressions, volatility, volume, and liquidity.
공개

2026년 11월 7일

Introduction

This post is a paper review of “Market Intraday Momentum” (2018) by Lei Gao, Yufeng Han, Sophia Zhengzi Li, and Guofu Zhou. This post mainly focuses on the methods used in the paper rather than the empirical results.

This paper presents the first study of market intraday time-series momentum, according to the authors.

They found that the first half-hour market return, measured from the previous day’s market close to 10:00 a.m., predicts the last half-hour market return.

They also showed that the predictability of the last half-hour return generally increases with volatility.

Therefore, the first half-hour return could be used as an indicator for predicting the last half-hour return.

1. Data and Return Calculation

The authors used high-frequency price data for SPY, an ETF tracking the S&P 500, from February 1, 1993, to December 31, 2013.

They divided the trading day into 13 half-hour periods.

The return for each period is calculated as

\[ r_{j,t} = \frac{P_{j,t}}{P_{j-1,t}}-1, \qquad j=1,\ldots,13 \]

where:

  • \(r_{j,t}\): Return for the \(j\)-th period on day \(t\)
  • \(P_{j,t}\): Price at the end of the \(j\)-th period
  • \(P_{j-1,t}\): Price at the end of the previous period

However, the first half-hour return is calculated from the previous trading day’s closing price rather than the current day’s opening price.

\[ P_{0,t}=P_{13,t-1} \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ r_{1,t} = \frac{P_{10:00,t}}{P_{16:00,t-1}}-1 } \]

This means that the first half-hour return includes the overnight return and the price movement from 9:30 a.m. to 10:00 a.m.

The authors mainly considered three returns:

Variable Time period Role
\(r_{1,t}\) Previous close to 10:00 a.m. Main predictor
\(r_{12,t}\) 3:00 p.m. to 3:30 p.m. Additional predictor
\(r_{13,t}\) 3:30 p.m. to 4:00 p.m. Prediction target

To examine volatility, they first calculated one-minute returns during the first half-hour.

Then, they computed realized volatility using these one-minute returns to estimate volatility during the first half-hour.

For reference, a conventional realized variance measure can be expressed as

\[ RV_{1,t} = \sum_{m=1}^{30} \left(r_{m,t}^{(1\mathrm{min})}\right)^2 \]

and realized volatility can be expressed as

\[ \sigma_{1,t}^{RV} = \sqrt{RV_{1,t}} \]

Here, \(r_{m,t}^{(1\mathrm{min})}\) denotes the return during minute \(m\) of the first half-hour.

Note: These are conventional realized-variance and realized-volatility formulas added for explanation. The paper describes the use of one-minute returns but does not explicitly present these exact equations.

2. Research Methodology

To identify intraday momentum, the authors used predictive regression analysis and conducted out-of-sample predictability tests.

They also examined the financial crisis period and analyzed volatility, trading volume, trade size, liquidity, and institutional trading.

2.1 Predictive Regression Analysis

In the predictive regression analysis, they first considered a regression of the last half-hour return on the first half-hour return.

The regression equation is

\[ \boxed{ r_{13,t} = \alpha+\beta r_{1,t}+\varepsilon_t } \]

where:

  • \(r_{13,t}\): Last half-hour return
  • \(r_{1,t}\): First half-hour return
  • \(\alpha\): Intercept
  • \(\beta\): Regression coefficient
  • \(\varepsilon_t\): Error term

A positive \(\beta\) indicates a positive relationship between the first and last half-hour returns.

Then, they added the twelfth half-hour return to the regression to see whether it improved the explanatory power of the first regression.

The extended regression is

\[ \boxed{ r_{13,t} = \alpha + \beta_1r_{1,t} + \beta_{12}r_{12,t} + \varepsilon_t } \]

The authors compared the \(R^2\) values of the two regressions.

The first regression achieved an in-sample \(R^2\) of 1.6%, while the regression using both predictors achieved an \(R^2\) of 2.6%.

This suggests that the twelfth half-hour return provides additional predictive information.

The paper also used Newey-West robust t-statistics to evaluate the statistical significance of the regression coefficients.

2.2 Out-of-Sample Predictability

In the out-of-sample predictability analysis, the authors conducted recursive regressions similar to the predictive regression analysis.

To forecast the last half-hour return on day \(t\), they estimated the regression coefficients using historical data available through day \(t-1\).

For the first-half-hour-only regression, the forecast is

\[ \widehat r_{13,t} = \widehat\alpha_{t-1} + \widehat\beta_{t-1}r_{1,t} \]

The coefficients are estimated from historical data, but \(r_{1,t}\) is observed on the same day before the last half-hour begins.

When the twelfth half-hour return is added, it is also observable by 3:30 p.m., before the prediction target period begins.

The authors initially estimated the model using observations through the end of 1997 and then expanded the estimation window by one month at a time.

To evaluate predictive performance, they used the out-of-sample \(R^2\):

\[ \boxed{ R^2_{OS} = 1- \frac{ \sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{OS}} (r_{13,t}-\widehat r_{13,t})^2 }{ \sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{OS}} (r_{13,t}-\overline r_{13,t})^2 } } \]

where:

  • \(r_{13,t}\): Actual last half-hour return
  • \(\widehat r_{13,t}\): Predicted return
  • \(\overline r_{13,t}\): Historical-average forecast estimated through \(t-1\)
  • \(\mathcal{T}_{OS}\): Out-of-sample evaluation period

If

\[ R^2_{OS}>0 \]

the predictive regression outperforms the historical-average benchmark in terms of squared forecast errors.

The authors reported an out-of-sample \(R^2\) of 1.4% using only the first half-hour return and 2.0% using both predictors.

This suggests that the predictability was not limited to the in-sample analysis.

2.3 Financial Crisis

For the financial crisis, the authors examined intraday momentum during the 2007–2009 financial crisis.

Conventional monthly momentum strategies are known to have performed poorly during this period.

However, the authors found that the predictive ability of the first half-hour return became stronger during the crisis.

They further showed that the predictive ability of the twelfth half-hour return also became stronger.

For example, the combined regression achieved an \(R^2\) of 6.9% during the crisis, compared with 1.1% when the crisis period was excluded.

The regression using only the twelfth half-hour return achieved an \(R^2\) of 3.1% during the crisis, compared with 0.3% outside the crisis period.

Therefore, the paper provides evidence of stronger intraday predictability during the financial crisis.

This does not necessarily imply that the twelfth half-hour return became more volatile than the first half-hour return.

Rather, it means that the twelfth half-hour return became a stronger predictor of the last half-hour return.

2.4 Volatility and Trading Volume

The authors found that the predictability of the last half-hour return generally increased with first half-hour realized volatility.

They divided trading days into three groups based on volatility:

  • Low volatility
  • Medium volatility
  • High volatility

Then, they estimated the predictive regressions separately for each group.

Using both predictors, the regression produced the following results:

Volatility group \(R^2\)
Low 0.6%
Medium 1.0%
High 3.3%

This provides evidence that first half-hour momentum is more strongly related to the last half-hour return on high-volatility days.

In terms of trading volume, the authors conducted a similar analysis using first half-hour trading volume.

They divided trading days into three volume groups within each year.

The results were as follows:

Trading volume group \(R^2\)
Low 1.1%
Medium 2.3%
High 3.1%

These results indicate that higher first half-hour trading volume was associated with greater overall predictability.

Thus, the paper does not support the conclusion that lower trading volume generally produces stronger intraday momentum.

2.5 Trade Size and Liquidity

Furthermore, the authors investigated trade size and liquidity.

These are related but different concepts.

Trade Size

The authors measured the average trade size during the last half-hour as

\[ \boxed{ TS_t = \frac{ V_{13,t} }{ N_{13,t} } } \]

where:

  • \(V_{13,t}\): Trading volume in the last half-hour
  • \(N_{13,t}\): Number of trades in the last half-hour

They divided trading days into small- and large-trade-size groups.

The predictive ability of the first half-hour return was relatively similar across the two groups.

The authors described the twelfth half-hour return as having stronger predictive ability on large-trade-size days.

However, the detailed regression results require a more careful interpretation.

For \(r_{12}\) alone:

Trade size group \(R^2\) t-statistic
Small trade size 1.0% 1.69
Large trade size 0.9% 2.53

The statistical significance is stronger on large-trade-size days, but the \(R^2\) values are similar, and the large-trade-size group does not have a higher \(R^2\).

Therefore, the results should not be interpreted as a uniform increase in explanatory power across all measures.

Liquidity

Next, the authors examined liquidity using the Amihud illiquidity measure.

They divided S&P 500 stocks into portfolios with low and high Amihud measures.

A high Amihud measure indicates lower liquidity.

The measure was calculated using the average ratio of absolute stock returns to dollar trading volume over the previous five trading days.

A mathematical representation is

\[ ILLIQ_{i,t} = \frac{1}{5} \sum_{k=1}^{5} \frac{|R_{i,t-k}|}{DV_{i,t-k}} \]

where:

  • \(R_{i,t-k}\): Return on stock \(i\)
  • \(DV_{i,t-k}\): Dollar trading volume
  • \(k=1,\ldots,5\)

The authors found that the predictive ability of the first half-hour return was relatively similar for high- and low-liquidity portfolios.

However, the predictive ability of the twelfth half-hour return was substantially stronger for less liquid stocks.

The results can be summarized as follows:

Predictor Low Amihud (more liquid) \(R^2\) High Amihud (less liquid) \(R^2\)
\(r_1\) 1.8% 2.3%
\(r_{12}\) 1.2% 5.9%
\(r_1\) and \(r_{12}\) 2.9% 8.1%

Therefore, less liquid stock portfolios showed greater overall predictability, particularly when the twelfth half-hour return was used as a predictor.

The authors suggested that predictability may be more difficult to exploit away in less liquid markets.

This means that predictable return patterns may persist because arbitrage is more difficult.

However, greater statistical predictability does not necessarily imply greater trading profitability, since lower liquidity may increase trading costs.

2.6 Institutional Trading

The authors also investigated the relationship between institutional trading and intraday momentum.

They analyzed institutional holdings and institutional order imbalances.

The institutional order imbalance was measured as

\[ \boxed{ OI_t = \frac{BV_t-SV_t}{BV_t+SV_t} } \]

where:

  • \(BV_t\): Institutional buy volume
  • \(SV_t\): Institutional sell volume

This is a signed measure of institutional net buying relative to total institutional trading volume.

A larger positive value indicates stronger net buying relative to selling.

It should not be confused with the absolute magnitude of the imbalance.

The authors found that the first half-hour return had relatively stable predictive ability across groups with different institutional holdings.

In contrast, the predictive ability of the twelfth half-hour return varied more substantially.

The results also showed stronger combined predictability on days with higher institutional order imbalance values.

The authors suggested that institutional trading near the market close may contribute to intraday momentum.

However, the institutional trading data did not cover all institutions, so the results should be interpreted as supporting evidence rather than definitive proof of causation.

3. Economic Significance

The paper also evaluated whether the predictive relationship could be used for market timing.

The first strategy takes a long position in the last half-hour when the first half-hour return is positive and a short position otherwise.

\[ \eta_t(r_1) = \begin{cases} r_{13,t}, & r_{1,t}>0\\ -r_{13,t}, & r_{1,t}\leq0 \end{cases} \]

The position is opened at 3:30 p.m. and closed at 4:00 p.m.

The authors also considered a strategy using both the first and twelfth half-hour returns.

\[ \eta_t(r_1,r_{12}) = \begin{cases} r_{13,t}, & r_{1,t}>0,\ r_{12,t}>0\\ -r_{13,t}, & r_{1,t}\leq0,\ r_{12,t}\leq0\\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

This second strategy trades only when both predictors indicate the same direction.

The first-half-hour-only strategy produced an annualized average return of 6.67% and a Sharpe ratio of 1.08 over the full sample.

The authors also examined transaction costs.

For the post-July 2001 sample, the first-half-hour strategy’s average annualized return was 4.46% after accounting for the bid-ask spread, before the separately evaluated commission and fee estimates.

This result uses a different sample period from the full-sample 6.67% figure, so the two numbers should not be directly subtracted to estimate transaction costs.

Overall, the results suggest that intraday momentum can have economic value, but practical profitability depends on trading costs and implementation conditions.

4. Explanations of Intraday Momentum

The paper suggests two possible economic explanations for the observed intraday momentum.

The first is infrequent portfolio rebalancing.

Some institutional investors may rebalance their portfolios near the market open, while others may execute similar trades near the market close.

These trading patterns may generate positive correlations between the first and last half-hour returns.

The second explanation is late-informed trading.

Some investors may receive or process information later than others.

If they trade near the market close in response to information that already affected prices earlier in the day, their trading activity may contribute to intraday momentum.

These explanations are consistent with the empirical results, but the paper does not establish them as the only possible causal mechanisms.

5. Additional Findings

The authors also examined several robustness checks.

First, they found that the first half-hour return had stronger predictive power on days when the first half-hour return was positive than on days when it was negative.

Second, they separated the first half-hour return into the overnight return and the return from 9:30 a.m. to 10:00 a.m.

They found that much of the predictive ability came from the overnight return.

They also examined alternative measurement endpoints at five-minute intervals from the market open to 10:00 a.m. and found relatively similar predictive results.

Therefore, this analysis does not establish that shorter time intervals necessarily produce better predictions.

Finally, the authors examined ten additional actively traded ETFs and found evidence of intraday momentum beyond SPY.

6. Personal Insight

My personal insight from this review is that the beginning of the trading day may provide important information for intraday trading.

The paper suggests that the market’s initial direction is positively related to the return during the last half-hour.

However, this does not mean that the trend necessarily continues throughout the entire trading day.

I initially thought that shorter time intervals and lower trading volume might be better for intraday trading.

However, the empirical results do not directly support this interpretation.

Instead, the authors found stronger overall predictability on days with higher first half-hour volatility and trading volume.

At the same time, they found greater predictability in less liquid stock portfolios, particularly for the twelfth half-hour return.

These are different results because trading volume and liquidity are not interchangeable measures.

Another important finding is that the overnight return contributes substantially to the predictive ability of the first half-hour return.

Thus, I think it would be useful to consider the information reflected in prices before the market opens, as well as the initial market return, volatility, and trading volume.

It may also be important to consider the last half-hour return in relation to the twelfth half-hour return, especially under different liquidity conditions.

However, statistical predictability does not guarantee profitable trading. Transaction costs, market liquidity, and unexpected news should also be considered.

Finally, since the sample period ends in 2013, the results would need to be tested using more recent market data before being applied to a current trading strategy.
