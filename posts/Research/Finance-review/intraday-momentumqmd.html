Paper Review - Market Intraday Momentum

Finance
Intraday
A review of the methodology used to identify and evaluate market intraday momentum.
공개

2026년 11월 7일

Introduction

This post is a paper review of “Market Intraday Momentum” (2018) by Lei Gao, Yufeng Han, Sophia Zhengzi Li, and Guofu Zhou. This post focuses mainly on the methods used in the paper rather than the empirical results.

This paper provides one of the first studies of intraday time-series momentum. The authors found that the first half-hour market return, measured from the previous day’s market close to 10:00 a.m., predicts the last half-hour market return.

They also showed that the predictability of the last half-hour return generally increases with volatility. Therefore, the returns during the first and last half-hour periods could be useful indicators of intraday momentum.

1. Data and Return Calculation

The authors used high-frequency data for SPY, an ETF tracking the S&P 500, from February 1993 to December 2013.

They divided each trading day into 13 half-hour periods.

The return for each period is calculated as follows:

\[ \boxed{ r_{j,t}=\frac{P_{j,t}}{P_{j-1,t}}-1 } \]

where:

  • \(r_{j,t}\): Return of the \(j\)-th half-hour period on day \(t\)
  • \(P_{j,t}\): Price at the end of the \(j\)-th period
  • \(P_{j-1,t}\): Price at the end of the previous period
  • \(j=1,\ldots,13\)

However, the first half-hour return is defined differently from an ordinary 30-minute return.

For the first period,

\[ P_{0,t}=P_{13,t-1} \]

which is the previous trading day’s closing price.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ r_{1,t} = \frac{P_{10:00,t}}{P_{16:00,t-1}}-1 } \]

This means that the first half-hour return includes both the overnight return and the return from 9:30 a.m. to 10:00 a.m.

The two main predictors considered in the paper are:

Variable Period Role
\(r_{1,t}\) Previous close to 10:00 a.m. Main predictor
\(r_{12,t}\) 3:00 p.m. to 3:30 p.m. Additional predictor
\(r_{13,t}\) 3:30 p.m. to 4:00 p.m. Prediction target

To examine the relationship between volatility and intraday momentum, they first calculated one-minute returns within the first half-hour.

They then computed realized volatility using these one-minute returns to estimate volatility during the first half-hour.

A conventional representation of this calculation is:

\[ RV_{1,t} = \sum_{m=1}^{30} \left(r_{m,t}^{(1\mathrm{min})}\right)^2 \]

and the corresponding realized volatility is

\[ \widehat{\sigma}_{1,t}^{RV} = \sqrt{RV_{1,t}} \]

Here, \(r_{m,t}^{(1\mathrm{min})}\) represents the return of the \(m\)-th minute during the first half-hour.

Note: This is a standard mathematical representation of realized volatility. The paper describes its calculation using one-minute returns but does not explicitly present this formula.

2. Research Methodology

To identify intraday momentum, the authors used predictive regression analysis and conducted an out-of-sample predictability test.

They also examined the financial crisis period, the effects of volatility and trading volume, trade size and liquidity, and institutional trading.

2.1 Predictive Regression Analysis

In the predictive regression analysis, the authors first considered a regression of the last half-hour return on the first half-hour return.

The regression equation is

\[ \boxed{ r_{13,t} = \alpha+\beta r_{1,t}+\varepsilon_t } \]

where:

  • \(r_{13,t}\): Last half-hour return
  • \(r_{1,t}\): First half-hour return
  • \(\alpha\): Intercept
  • \(\beta\): Regression coefficient
  • \(\varepsilon_t\): Error term

The coefficient \(\beta\) measures the relationship between the first and last half-hour returns.

If

\[ \beta>0 \]

then a positive first half-hour return tends to be associated with a positive last half-hour return.

They then added the twelfth half-hour return to the regression to see whether it improves the predictive \(R^2\) of the first regression.

The extended regression is

\[ \boxed{ r_{13,t} = \alpha + \beta_1r_{1,t} + \beta_{12}r_{12,t} + \varepsilon_t } \]

The authors compared the explanatory power of the two models.

The first model achieved an in-sample \(R^2\) of 1.6%, while the model using both predictors achieved an \(R^2\) of 2.6%.

This suggests that the twelfth half-hour return provides additional predictive information beyond the first half-hour return.

2.2 Out-of-Sample Predictability

In the out-of-sample predictability analysis, the authors conducted recursive regressions similar to the predictive regression analysis.

To forecast the return on day \(t\), they used regression coefficients estimated from data available through day \(t-1\).

For the first half-hour predictor, the forecast can be written as

\[ \widehat{r}_{13,t} = \widehat{\alpha}_{t-1} + \widehat{\beta}_{t-1}r_{1,t} \]

Here, the regression coefficients are estimated using historical data, while the first half-hour return on day \(t\) is observed before the final half-hour trading period begins.

To evaluate the forecasting performance, the authors used the out-of-sample \(R^2\):

\[ \boxed{ R^2_{OS} = 1- \frac{ \sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{OS}} (r_{13,t}-\widehat{r}_{13,t})^2 }{ \sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{OS}} (r_{13,t}-\overline{r}_{13,t})^2 } } \]

where:

  • \(r_{13,t}\): Actual last half-hour return
  • \(\widehat{r}_{13,t}\): Forecast from the predictive regression
  • \(\overline{r}_{13,t}\): Historical mean forecast based on data through \(t-1\)
  • \(\mathcal{T}_{OS}\): Out-of-sample evaluation period

The initial estimation sample ended in December 1997, and the estimation window was expanded by one month at a time.

A positive \(R^2_{OS}\) means that the predictive regression performs better than the historical-average benchmark.

The authors found positive out-of-sample \(R^2\) values for both the single-predictor and two-predictor models.

This provides evidence that the predictability was not limited to the in-sample regression results.

2.3 Financial Crisis

For the financial crisis, the authors examined intraday momentum during the 2007–2009 crisis period.

Conventional monthly momentum strategies are known to have performed poorly during this crisis.

However, the authors found that the predictive relationship between the first and last half-hour returns became stronger during the crisis.

The twelfth half-hour return also showed greater predictive power during the crisis.

For example, the \(R^2\) of the regression using both predictors increased to 6.9% during the financial crisis, compared with 1.1% when the crisis period was excluded.

This suggests that intraday momentum may become more pronounced during periods of market stress.

However, this result does not necessarily mean that the twelfth half-hour return itself became more volatile than the first half-hour return.

Rather, its ability to predict the last half-hour return became stronger.

2.4 Volatility and Trading Volume

The increase in volatility generally improved the predictability of the last half-hour return.

The authors divided trading days into three groups according to first half-hour realized volatility:

  • Low volatility
  • Medium volatility
  • High volatility

They then estimated the predictive regression separately for each group.

Using both predictors, the \(R^2\) increased from 0.6% in the low-volatility group to 3.3% in the high-volatility group.

This provides evidence that the first half-hour return has stronger predictive power for the last half-hour return on more volatile days.

In terms of trading volume, they conducted a similar analysis.

Trading days were divided into three groups based on first half-hour trading volume, with the grouping performed within each year.

The authors found that the combined regression’s predictive \(R^2\) increased with trading volume.

Therefore, higher trading volume, rather than lower trading volume, was associated with stronger intraday predictability in this analysis.

2.5 Trade Size and Liquidity

Furthermore, the authors examined trade size and liquidity.

First, they estimated average trade size during the last half-hour using

\[ \boxed{ \mathrm{TradeSize}_t = \frac{ \mathrm{Volume}_{13,t} }{ \mathrm{NumberOfTrades}_{13,t} } } \]

This measure represents the average number of shares per trade.

It is different from total trading volume.

The authors divided trading days into small- and large-trade-size groups and estimated the predictive regressions separately.

They found that the predictive power of the first half-hour return was relatively stable across the two groups.

However, the predictive power of the twelfth half-hour return was stronger on large-trade-size days.

Next, they examined liquidity using the Amihud illiquidity measure.

A conventional expression of the measure is

\[ ILLIQ_{i,d} = \frac{1}{5} \sum_{k=1}^{5} \frac{ |R_{i,d-k}| }{ DV_{i,d-k} } \]

where:

  • \(R_{i,d-k}\): Daily return of stock \(i\)
  • \(DV_{i,d-k}\): Dollar trading volume
  • The measure uses the previous five trading days

A higher Amihud measure indicates lower liquidity.

The predictive ability of the first half-hour return remained relatively similar between high- and low-liquidity portfolios.

However, the twelfth half-hour return showed stronger predictive power for less liquid stocks.

This suggests that liquidity affects the two intraday momentum predictors differently.

2.6 Institutional Trading

The authors also examined whether institutional trading contributes to intraday momentum.

They analyzed institutional holdings and institutional order imbalances.

The institutional order imbalance measure is defined as

\[ \boxed{ OI_t = \frac{ BV_t-SV_t }{ BV_t+SV_t } } \]

where:

  • \(BV_t\): Institutional buy volume
  • \(SV_t\): Institutional sell volume

A positive order imbalance indicates that institutional buying exceeds selling.

The results suggest that institutional trading activity is more closely associated with the predictive power of the twelfth half-hour return than with that of the first half-hour return.

In particular, predictability was stronger on days with higher institutional order imbalances.

However, institutional activity was only partially observed, so these findings should not be interpreted as direct causal evidence.

3. Economic Significance

The authors also investigated whether intraday predictability could be used in a trading strategy.

One simple strategy uses the sign of the first half-hour return to determine the position during the last half-hour.

The strategy return is defined as

\[ \eta_t(r_1) = \begin{cases} r_{13,t}, & r_{1,t}>0\\ -r_{13,t}, & r_{1,t}\leq0 \end{cases} \]

If the first half-hour return is positive, the strategy takes a long position during the last half-hour.

Otherwise, it takes a short position.

The position is closed at the end of the trading day.

The authors also considered a strategy using both the first and twelfth half-hour returns.

\[ \eta_t(r_1,r_{12}) = \begin{cases} r_{13,t}, & r_{1,t}>0,\ r_{12,t}>0\\ -r_{13,t}, & r_{1,t}\leq0,\ r_{12,t}\leq0\\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

In this strategy, a position is taken only when both predictors indicate the same direction.

The first-half-hour-only strategy generated an annualized average return of 6.67% and a Sharpe ratio of 1.08 in the paper’s sample.

The authors also examined bid-ask spreads, commissions, and other transaction costs.

The results indicate that intraday momentum can have economic value, although transaction costs reduce the realized profitability.

4. Explanations of Intraday Momentum

The paper suggests two possible explanations for intraday momentum.

The first is infrequent portfolio rebalancing.

Institutional investors may adjust their portfolios at different times during the trading day. Some may trade near the market open, while others may execute similar trades near the market close.

This may generate a positive relationship between the first and last half-hour returns.

The second explanation is late-informed trading.

Some investors may receive or process information later than others.

These investors may trade near the market close in response to information that had already affected prices near the market open.

As a result, trading in the same direction near the close may contribute to intraday momentum.

These are proposed economic explanations consistent with the empirical evidence, rather than definitive proof of causality.

5. Personal Insight

My personal insight from this review is that the beginning of the trading day may provide useful information for intraday trading.

However, the paper does not necessarily imply that shorter time intervals or lower trading volume are always better.

Instead, the results suggest that intraday momentum is generally stronger on days with higher opening volatility and trading volume.

Another important point is that the first half-hour return includes overnight information. Therefore, the information reflected in prices before the market opens may be particularly important.

The early market trend is also more likely to be related to the final half-hour return, although this does not mean that the trend must persist throughout the entire day.

Thus, it may be useful to consider the market’s initial return, opening volatility, and trading volume rather than relying only on the entire intraday price trajectory.

For practical trading, I would also consider market liquidity, transaction costs, and unexpected news, since statistical predictability does not guarantee profitable trades.
